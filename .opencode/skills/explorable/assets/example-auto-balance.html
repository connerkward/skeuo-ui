How the auto-balance algorithm works

A layout has a portrait on one side and a column of text on the other. When does it feel balanced — and how do you get a machine to decide that without relying on its (untrustworthy) opinion? This is the whole pipeline, one playable idea at a time.

Every figure below is live. Drag things, move sliders, press the buttons. Nothing here is a screenshot — each widget runs the same math the real studio (asym.html) runs. Read it top to bottom; each idea is a brick for the next.

01 / FOUNDATIONThe see-saw: what "balance" physically is

Forget pixels for a second. A composition balances the way a playground see-saw balances. Each element has a visual weight and sits at some distance from the center line. What tips the beam is not weight alone — it's weight times distance. That product is the moment (physicists call it torque).

moment = Σ over every element i of ( weighti × distancei from center )
balanced ⇔ moment ≈ 0 (left moments cancel right moments)

A small weight far out can balance a big weight near the middle — which is exactly the trick the whole algorithm exploits later: a heavy portrait near the edge gets counterweighted by making the heading bigger, or by sliding the portrait inward (shorter lever arm).

drag either block left ↔ right
left moment — right moment — net — —

The text column (left, light + tall) vs the portrait (right, heavy + compact). Move them, or change the portrait's weight. The beam tilts with the net moment. Get it level.

Predict before you drag: the portrait weighs more than the text. To balance, should the portrait be closer to or farther from the center than the text?

Closer. The heavier element needs the shorter lever arm. That's why the final layout pulls the portrait inward (pShift) and never lets it sit at the far margin — distance is doing as much work as weight.
Pedagogy — concreteness fading (Jerome Bruner, 1966). We start enactive: you physically drag a beam. Later we move to iconic (centroid dots) and finally symbolic (the regression equations). Starting at the equation is how most explanations lose people. The physics metaphor is from Rudolf Arnheim, Art and Visual Perception (1954), the canonical text on visual weight and pictorial balance.

02 / CALIBRATIONThe center isn't the center

"Balanced around the middle" hides a subtlety. The optical center — where the eye perceives the middle — sits slightly above the geometric middle. Put something at exactly 50% height and it looks like it's sagging. Designers nudge it up; the studio uses y = 0.46 (46% down from the top) as the vertical target, and x = 0.50 horizontally.

OPT = { x: 0.50, y: 0.46 } // optical center — geometric x, slightly-high y
same dot, two ideas of "centered"

The dot is fixed at the true middle of the frame. Toggle the target line between geometric center and optical center. Against the optical line the dot reads as low — which is why the algorithm aims a hair higher.

Pedagogy — confront the misconception directly (Posner et al., 1982, conceptual change). "Center = 50%" is a sticky wrong intuition. Rather than just stating the correction, the widget makes you see the dot look wrong, creating the dissonance that makes the right rule stick.

03 / GENERALIZEFrom see-saw to centroid

The see-saw was one dimension (left–right). A page is two. Generalize the moment idea: the balance point of all the visual weight is the centroid — the weighted average position. It's literally the center of mass of the ink.

centroidx = Σ(wᵢ · xᵢ) / Σwᵢ    // weighted-average x
centroidy = Σ(wᵢ · yᵢ) / Σwᵢ    // weighted-average y

A layout is balanced when its centroid lands on the optical center from §2. Click in the frame below to drop weighted blobs and watch the centroid (the ◉) chase the center of mass. The blob size = its weight.

Two blobs on one side pull the centroid that way; one heavy blob equals two light ones at half the distance. Try to land the ◉ on the ✛.

You drop one big blob on the right. Where do you put one blob to re-center the ◉ — and how big?

Mirror it: same weight, symmetric position. Or a heavier blob closer to center, or a lighter blob farther out — any combination with an equal-and-opposite moment. There's no single answer, which is precisely why the auto-balancer has several levers to pull.

04 / THE HARD PARTWhat "weight" actually means: ink density

Here's where naïve balance breaks. Two boxes the same size are not the same weight. A solid black heading is heavy. The ASCII portrait — which looks like a big block — is actually a field of grey dither on cream paper, so per unit area it carries far less visual weight than it appears to. The studio encodes this as an ink-density multiplier per element type:

DENS = { portrait:0.34, h1:0.82, kicker:0.42, lead:0.22, body:0.16, meta:0.5 }
weight = area × width × density // big-but-grey ≠ small-but-black

This single table is what makes the model match reality. The first hand-guess gave the portrait density 0.62 — the model then claimed a layout was balanced that the pixels showed was badly lopsided. Pedagogy — a worked example with one varying part (Sweller, cognitive load theory, 1988). Geometry is frozen so your whole working memory goes to the one causal variable: density. Changing many things at once is how interactive demos overwhelm. Isolate the variable.

05 / TWO MODELSThe cheap estimate and the expensive truth

There are two ways to measure the centroid, and they disagree.

The box model (cheap, instant). Treat each element as its bounding box; put its weight at the box's center. Runs every frame as you drag a slider. But it has a built-in bug: text is left-aligned, so its ink sits toward the left of its box, not the box's center. The box model places the weight too far right.

The pixel oracle (expensive, true). Actually rasterize the layout to a canvas (html2canvas), then average the position of every non-paper pixel. This is the real ink centroid — no alignment bug — but it costs a full screenshot, so you only run it on demand (the 📸 pixels button).

The grey rectangle is a text block; the dark marks are where the ink really is. □ is what the box model assumes (block center); ◉ is the true ink centroid. Switch alignment to center and watch the gap collapse — the bug is alignment-dependent.

If you justified the text (ink spread evenly edge to edge), what happens to the gap between □ and ◉?

It nearly vanishes — justified/centered text has its ink centroid at the box center, so the box model becomes accurate. The gap is a systematic function of layout parameters, not random noise. That predictability is the key to §6–7: a systematic error can be learned and subtracted.

06 / THE FIX, PART 1Model the discrepancy, don't rebuild the model

The box model is wrong but cheap; the pixel oracle is right but slow. The classic move — straight out of Kennedy & O'Hagan (2001), "Bayesian calibration of computer models" — is to not throw away the cheap model. Instead, write reality as the cheap model plus a learned correction:

truth(x) ≈ cheap_model(x) + δ(x)
// box centroid + a discrepancy term that depends on the layout x

Every time you press 📸 pixels, the studio records one data point: the layout's parameters, and the gap δ = pixel_truth − box_estimate at that layout. The discrepancy isn't a single number — it shifts as the layout changes (more portrait → bigger right-bias, etc.). So δ is a function of the layout, and we learn that function from the samples. That's §7.

Pedagogy — name the expert move (Chi et al., 1989, self-explanation & expert schemas). Naming this "surrogate + discrepancy modeling" and attaching the Kennedy–O'Hagan citation gives you a transferable schema, not a one-off trick. You'll recognize the same pattern in ML residual correction and in engineering "fudge-factor" models.

07 / THE FIX, PART 2Learning δ with ridge regression

Each 📸 sample is (features → δ). The features are just the layout's normalized parameters: portrait size, pull-in, heading scale, gap, measure, which side, etc. We fit a linear map from features to δ. With only a handful of samples and nine features, a plain least-squares fit overfits wildly — it'll draw an absurd steep line through 3 points and explode on the 4th.

Ridge regression fixes this by adding a penalty λ on the size of the weights. Large λ shrinks the fit toward zero slope — which here means "when in doubt, trust the cheap model and apply no correction." The studio uses λ = 0.08: enough to stay stable on 2–4 samples, small enough to still bend toward the data.

w = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹ Xᵀy   // the ridge normal equations
δ̂(x) = w · features(x)   // predicted discrepancy at a new layout

Below is the idea in 1D (one feature, e.g. heading scale → δ). Click to add sample points; drag the λ slider. Watch a small λ chase every point (overfit) and a large λ flatten toward the safe "no correction" line.

The line is the learned δ̂. Dashed grey = the "no correction" line (δ=0). As λ→large the fit relaxes onto the dashed line; as λ→0 it strains to hit every dot, even a noisy one.

Two layouts gave almost the same box estimate but very different pixel truths. With λ near zero, what does the fitted slope do — and why is that bad?

It blows up — near-identical x with different y forces an enormous slope (this literally happened: the scalar fit hit slope ≈ 32). Ridge's λ penalty is exactly the guard against that degeneracy. It was the fix after the variance blow-up.

08 / THE DIALTrusting the correction: α

The learned δ is only as good as its few samples. So the studio doesn't force you to fully trust it — it gives you a dial, α, that blends the raw cheap estimate with the corrected one:

centroid = raw + α · δ̂ // α=0 → pure box model · α=1 → full correction

At α = 0 you see the cheap model alone. At α = 1 you fully apply the learned discrepancy. (The studio auto-bumps α to 1 the first time you take a pixel sample — once there's any ground truth, start trusting it.) The dial exists because this is a hand-tunable trade-off, not a fact: you, the human, decide how much to believe the model.

□ raw   ◆ corrected   ◉ blended (what you act on)
raw 0.560 +α·δ — blended —

Raw box says 0.560 (too far right). The pixel-learned δ pulls it toward 0.50. Slide α to blend. The ◉ is the value the rest of the system uses.

09 / THE LOOPAuto-balance: gradient-free hill climbing

Now put it together. "Auto-balance" doesn't solve an equation — it nudges. It measures the imbalance, decides which way the centroid is pulled, and applies the cheapest counterweight first, escalating only if needed. The ordering is deliberate, lightest-touch to heaviest:

while imbalance > tol && steps < 120:
  if centroid pulled toward portrait:
    1. grow the heading (counterweight the text side) headScale → 2.2
    2. else turn on the meta block more text-side weight
    3. else pull the portrait inward pShift → shorter lever
    4. else shrink the portrait last resort: less weight
  else (overshot): unwind in reverse

It's a bounded, gradient-free hill-climb with a step guard (max 120 iterations) so it can't loop forever. Press run and watch it converge — the log shows which lever it reached for each step.

A live see-saw of the actual layout. Watch it grow the heading first (cheap counterweight), then pull the portrait in, until the centroid sits on the axis.

Pedagogy — synthesis after segmenting (Mayer, 2005, multimedia principles). Concepts were taught one at a time (§1–8); only now do they combine. Recombining isolated parts into the whole is where durable understanding forms — but only after each part is solid on its own.

10 / THE POINTWhy any of this exists

The honest version: a language model has no reliable sense of visual balance. Asked "does this look centered?", it confabulates. The entire apparatus above exists to replace opinion with a measured number — and then to keep that number honest with an independent check (the pixel oracle), because a model that grades its own homework always passes.

That's the load-bearing idea, and it generalizes far past page layout:

  • Don't trust a metric that shares the assumptions of the thing it measures. The box model judging the box model is circular. The pixel oracle is independent — it rasterizes and counts, knowing nothing about the layout's intent.
  • Cheap-model-plus-learned-correction (§6) beats both "use the cheap wrong thing" and "always pay for the expensive thing."
  • Keep a human dial (§8) on anything where the model's confidence outruns its evidence.

And the caveat the studio prints to itself, in code: HUD_APPROX = true — "the live HUD is an estimate; the pixel measurement on a screenshot is ground truth." The cheap model is a guide, never the verdict. When the two disagree, the pixels win.

Final check: why couldn't we just ask a vision model "rate this layout's balance 1–10" and skip all the math?

Because that score isn't independent of the thing being judged, isn't reproducible, and gives no gradient — it can't tell you which lever to move or by how much. The centroid offset is a signed, decomposable number (too-far-right by 0.06): it points at the fix. A vibe score doesn't. This is the same independence principle as not validating a tilt-corrector with the same tilt model that drove the correction.
